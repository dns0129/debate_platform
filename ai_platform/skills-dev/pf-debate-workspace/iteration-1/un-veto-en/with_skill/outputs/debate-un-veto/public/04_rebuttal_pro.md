Con's case stands on one link. Take away the veto, and the great powers walk out or go to war. Every impact they have runs through it: the League, peacekeeping, nuclear war. I'll break it with counterexamples, most of them from Con's own evidence.

On their first contention, keeping the giants in the room.

Start with the League. Davies and Bellamy say the major powers were gone by the Second World War [C1]. In crossfire, Con conceded the card "doesn't give the reason for each departure." It can't be a missing veto. The League decided by unanimity. Every member at its table could block. The giants left anyway. So a veto for everyone did not keep them in the room.

Next, David Bosco's universal claim: "the alternative to the Security Council veto is really no Security Council" [C4]. One counterexample breaks a universal claim. Here are two. France's foreign ministry states this month, on France Diplomatie, that France committed in 2013 not to use its veto in mass atrocities, that the United Kingdom recently joined that pledge, and that France has not used its veto since 1989 [P9]. Thirty-seven years without casting one. Did France leave? No. It is publishing proposals to strengthen the UN.

Con asks why Washington, Moscow or Beijing would stay. Answer: because the seat is worth more than the veto. Under our plan they keep their permanent seats, their votes, and the power to build a nine-vote majority. Walking out trades all that for nothing.

In crossfire, Con drew one line: losing a non-binding vote is cheap, but losing a binding one makes you leave. Their own card erases that line. The Moscow Times called the court's March 2022 order "binding," and the Kremlin answered, "We can't take that decision into account" [C9]. A great power lost a binding decision. Did it leave the United Nations? No. It stayed and ignored the order. That is not exit.

Last, peacekeeping. We agree with Walter, Howard and Fortna that peacekeepers bring peace [C6]. But what blocks the Council today is not an empty chamber. It is a vetoed one. The Assembly President listed Mali among the crises the Council "remains unable to collectively address" [P5]. Peacekeepers need a decision. We make decisions possible.

On their second contention, the safety valve.

Con says nine votes bind Russia, Russia ignores it, and then what: war or retreat? Their own card answers. After the court's binding order, nobody went to war to enforce it. There is a third option. The decision stands, and under Article 25 it binds every other member, including everyone who trades with Russia [P6]. An authorization permits states to act; it compels none of them.

Con also asks why a vote would stop Russia's army. It wouldn't, and we never said it would. And an aid mandate like Bab al-Hawa needs no enforcement at all.

Now Stewart Patrick: no structure of peace is viable if war can be authorized "contrary to the expressed wishes" of a great power [C3]. Counterexample. UN News reported in March 2011 that the Council, acting under Chapter Seven, authorized "all necessary measures" in Libya by ten votes to zero, with China and Russia, both veto holders, abstaining [P8]. Force was authorized without their support. No great-power war. No exit. And if Con calls Libya overreach, notice that it passed with the veto in place.

Then the turn. Patrick says the system is "rigged to advance the interests of the great powers." That is the problem. In 2022 the veto did not stop a permanent member from invading its neighbor [P2]. It stopped the response. The safety valve vents for the aggressor.

So weigh it. Con says their impact comes first: without the giants, nothing passes. But with the veto, nothing passes either, not on Gaza, Syria, Ukraine, Mali or North Korea. Nuclear war is irreversible, yes, but their own evidence shows a great power ignoring a binding decision, not fighting over it. Their harm is a prediction. Ours is a voting record. Thirteen of fifteen for a lifeline to four million people [P3]. Fourteen for a Gaza ceasefire [P4]. One no each time. Please vote Pro.
