Resolved: The United Nations should abolish the permanent members' veto power in the Security Council. My partner and I affirm.

Let's be precise about what changes. Article 27 of the United Nations Charter says substantive decisions need nine votes "including the concurring votes of the permanent members" [P1]. Abolishing the veto strikes that clause and nothing else. China, France, Russia, the United Kingdom and the United States keep their permanent seats and their votes. A resolution still needs nine of fifteen. We are not creating a UN army. We are ending the power of one country to cancel the other fourteen.

How should you judge this round? By the Council's own job description. Articles 24 and 25 of the same United Nations Charter give the Council primary responsibility for peace and security "to ensure prompt and effective action," and bind every member to carry out its decisions [P6]. So the question is simple: which rule lets the Council act when it should?

Our case has two contentions, and the second builds on the first. First, the veto blocks help. Second, it blocks accountability, because the country holding the veto is often the country on trial.

Contention one: the veto blocks help.

This is not a rare problem. Between a 2022 General Assembly resolution and April 2024, vetoes were used 13 times, and that April UN News reported the Assembly President's warning that the Council "remains unable to collectively address" Gaza, Mali, Syria, Ukraine and North Korea [P5].

Take one vote. In July 2023, the Council voted on renewing the Bab al-Hawa crossing, which UN News called "a lifeline for four million people in northwest Syria." Thirteen of fifteen members voted yes. It was struck down by a single no vote from Russia [P3]. Under our rule, thirteen is more than nine, and the aid mandate continues.

And this is not about one bad actor. Fourteen members backed a Gaza ceasefire resolution in September 2025 as famine spread; UN News reported that the United States vetoed it, its sixth veto of a ceasefire in that war [P4]. Different country, same result: the world agrees, and one capital says no.

Contention two: the veto blocks accountability.

Here is the design flaw. Article 27 requires a party to a dispute to abstain, but only in decisions under Chapter Six, the peaceful-settlement chapter [P1]. Nothing makes the accused step aside when the Council considers enforcement under Chapter Seven. In February 2022, UN News reported that Russia vetoed a resolution demanding that it stop its attack on Ukraine and withdraw, even though eleven members voted in favor [P2]. The defendant was also the judge.

Why would a resolution matter if Russia ignores it? Because Council decisions bind all 193 members, not just the aggressor. Today, sanctions come from blocs, and major buyers can opt out. In July 2025, The Tribune reported India's foreign ministry saying India "does not subscribe to any unilateral sanction measures," responding to European sanctions on Russian energy [P10]. A Council decision is not unilateral; it is an obligation every member accepted under Article 25. The veto is the difference between a coalition of some countries and a rule for all of them.

So the contentions build. The veto blocks action, and it blocks it exactly where the stakes are highest: when a great power, or its ally, is doing the harm. Better diplomacy cannot fix a rule working as written.

Finally, weighing. Our opponents will say the veto keeps great powers at the table and prevents war between them. But the veto did not stop a permanent member from invading its neighbor in 2022; it only stopped the response. And being outvoted does not make great powers walk out. In March 2022, UN News reported that Russia lost a General Assembly vote on its own invasion, 141 to 5 [P7], and Russia still sits on the Council today. Their harm is a prediction. Ours is a voting record: a lifeline for four million people vetoed, a famine, and an invasion the Council could not formally answer.

We are proud to affirm.
