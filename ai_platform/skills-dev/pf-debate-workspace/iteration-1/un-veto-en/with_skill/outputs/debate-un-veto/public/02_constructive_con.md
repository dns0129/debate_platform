In 1944, at the Dumbarton Oaks conference where the United Nations was being designed, an American senator complained to Secretary of State Cordell Hull that the Security Council plan was "a discrimination against small nations." According to Stewart Patrick of the Council on Foreign Relations, writing in 2016, Hull replied bluntly that the United States "would not remain there for a day without retaining its veto power" [C2].

That exchange is this whole debate in miniature. The veto is not fair, and we will not pretend it is. But the UN was built for one job above all: keeping the peace among countries that can destroy each other. So the question is simple. Which Security Council keeps more peace: one that works through the great powers, or one that tries to outvote them? We negate with two contentions: keeping the giants in the room, and the safety valve.

Contention one: keeping the giants in the room.

The UN's predecessor tried it the other way. Writing in The Conversation in 2014, Griffith University scholars Sara Davies and Alex Bellamy note that by the onset of the Second World War, "none of the most significant world powers (US, USSR, Germany, Japan) were members" of the League of Nations [C1]. Whatever else went wrong, the countries that mattered were not in the room when it counted.

Indiana University professor David Bosco put the principle plainly in Foreign Policy in 2012: because great powers will not accept binding authority unless they are certain it will not prejudice their interests, "the alternative to the Security Council veto is really no Security Council" [C4].

This is not just history. In January 2026, with the veto still in place, Anadolu Agency reported that the United States decided to withdraw from 31 UN-linked entities, part of 66 organizations the administration argued "are no longer serving American interests" [C5]. If Washington leaves UN bodies it says no longer serve it, what does it do when nine countries can outvote it on sanctions and war? Pro must explain why Washington, Moscow or Beijing would stay.

And losing them costs lives. When the great powers agree, the Council sends peacekeepers. A 2021 review in the British Journal of Political Science by Barbara Walter, Lise Howard and Page Fortna reviewed numerous empirical studies and found their results "reveal an extraordinary relationship between peacekeepers and peace" [C6]. A Council the great powers have abandoned sends no one.

Contention two: the safety valve.

In March 2022, the International Court of Justice ordered Russia to "immediately suspend" its offensive in Ukraine. The Moscow Times reported that the ruling was "binding but lacks any real means to enforce it," and the Kremlin answered: "We can't take that decision into account" [C9].

Now a hypothetical. Picture that month in a Council with no veto. Nine members vote to impose binding measures on Russia and authorize force. Under Article 25 of the United Nations Charter, every member agrees "to accept and carry out the decisions of the Security Council" [C7]. Russia ignores the Council, just as it ignored the court. Then what? Either the UN backs down and proves its orders are empty, or states enforce them against a nuclear power.

The founders refused to create that choice. In the same 2016 piece, Stewart Patrick explains the rationale: "no structure of peace could be viable if war could be authorized contrary to the expressed wishes of one of the world's policemen" [C3]. The system, he adds, is "rigged to advance the interests of the great powers—and to reduce the likelihood that major war will break out among them."

So how should you weigh? Pro will describe vetoed resolutions on Syria, Ukraine and Gaza, and those are real tragedies. But ask two questions. If Russia already ignores a binding court order, why would a vote stop its army? And what is left to vote on once the great powers leave? Our impacts come first: without the giants in the room, nothing Pro wants can be passed or enforced, and war between nuclear powers is the one harm no resolution can undo. Please negate.
