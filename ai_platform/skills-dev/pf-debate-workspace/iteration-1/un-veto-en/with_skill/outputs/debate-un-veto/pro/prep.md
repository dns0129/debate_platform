# Pro prep - UN veto (private: Pro 1 The Architect, Pro 2 The Counterexample Hunter)

## Core question
Does the Security Council do its Charter job - "prompt and effective action" for peace and security on behalf of all members (Art. 24, [P6]) - better when a single permanent member can no longer block a decision alone?
- Pro must prove: the veto blocks action that would otherwise pass and that matters, and removing it does more good than the risks Con names.
- Con must prove: the veto prevents something worse (great-power exit, great-power war, reckless majorities), or abolition changes nothing.

## Framework (say it the same way every speech)
Judge by the Council's own job description: which rule lets the Council act when it should? [P6]
Definition (narrow, hold it all round): abolition = strike "including the concurring votes of the permanent members" from Art. 27(3) [P1]. P5 keep permanent seats and votes. Decisions still need 9 of 15. No UN army, no Council expansion. If Con attacks "permanent seats" or "expansion", that is not our resolution.

## Contention 1 - The veto blocks help
- Uniqueness: Council unable to act on Gaza, Mali, Syria, Ukraine, DPRK; vetoes kept coming after the 2022 GA resolution [P5].
- Link: 13-of-15 Syria aid renewal killed by one no [P3]; 14-of-15 Gaza ceasefire killed by one no [P4]. Under Pro, 9 votes suffice -> both pass. Pure arithmetic - the cleanest link in the round.
- Impact: a lifeline for four million people [P3]; famine conditions in Gaza [P4].
- Structural point: two different vetoers (Russia, US) -> not a "bad actor" problem, it's the rule.

## Contention 2 - The veto blocks accountability (judge in its own case)
- Link: Art. 27 makes a party abstain only under Ch. VI [P1]; under Ch. VII enforcement, the accused votes. Russia vetoed the resolution on its own invasion [P2].
- Internal link (what a passed resolution changes): Council decisions bind all 193 members [P6]. Today sanctions come from blocs, and big buyers opt out - India rejects "unilateral" sanctions [P10]. A Council decision is not unilateral. Veto = difference between a coalition and a global rule.
- Impact: the norm against invasion only binds those without a veto or a veto-holding friend. Weakest link: "Russia would ignore it anyway." Answer: the resolution isn't aimed only at Russia; it binds everyone who trades with Russia [P6][P10]. Do NOT claim a resolution would have stopped the tanks.

## Blocks (for Pro 2 especially - one counterexample per universal claim)

1. **"Without the veto, great powers leave the UN / League of Nations."** (defense)
   - Counterexample: Russia lost 141-5 in the GA [P7] and is still in the UN, still on the Council, still casting vetoes (P4 page shows 2025-26 Council activity; P5 shows Russia's deputy speaking in the GA in 2024).
   - Counterexample: France has not used its veto since 1989 [P9] - a permanent member stays fully engaged without ever vetoing. The value of the seat isn't the veto.
   - Our definition keeps permanent seats: leaving means giving up the seat, the GA vote, the agencies. Exit costs more than being outvoted.
   - League: analytics only. The League's Covenant required unanimity for most decisions - it had more vetoes, not fewer, and still failed. CAUTION: do not say "Japan left despite having a veto" - Japan walked out in 1933 after the Lytton report was adopted over its objection (parties to a dispute were excluded from that vote). If Con raises Japan, pivot: the League also lacked the US entirely and had no binding sanctions obligation like Art. 25.

2. **"The veto prevents great-power war / is a safety valve."** (defense + light turn)
   - Counterexample: the veto did not prevent a permanent member invading its neighbor in 2022 [P2]. It stopped the response, not the war.
   - Nuclear deterrence, not Art. 27, keeps P5 from fighting each other.
   - A majority resolution conscripts nobody. The Council has never had a standing army; states choose whether to contribute forces. "UN war against Russia" is not the Pro world.
   - Turn (use sparingly): guaranteed impunity lowers the cost of aggression for 5 states and their clients.

3. **"Majority tyranny / Western overreach (Libya, Iraq)."** (defense)
   - Counterexample: Libya 1973 passed 10-0-5 WITH the veto in place; China and Russia abstained rather than block it [P8]. The veto did not stop the case Con fears.
   - Iraq 2003: the US acted without Council authorization - the veto doesn't restrain powers who go around the Council (analytic, common knowledge).
   - The "majority" is not a bloc: 13 of 15 on Syria aid [P3] included the US, UK and France; 14 of 15 on Gaza [P4] included Russia and China. Majorities change by issue.
   - 9 of 15 still needs cross-regional votes; elected members come from every region (analytic).

4. **"Resolutions are paper; unenforceable against great powers."** (defense)
   - Syria aid: the resolution IS the action - the mandate is the legal basis for UN cross-border delivery [P3]. No enforcement needed.
   - Binding on all members [P6]; unilateral sanctions let major buyers opt out [P10].
   - Note: Russia's own GA line (on P5's page, not in our quote) is that without a veto the Council would "rubberstamp" decisions "practically impossible to implement". Expect Con to read something like it. Answer = this block.

5. **"Reform already exists: veto initiative / France-Mexico restraint - so abolition is unnecessary."** (defense + turn of their evidence)
   - Vetoes kept coming after the 2022 GA resolution - 13 by April 2024 - and GA resolutions are non-binding [P5].
   - Only 2 of 5 P5 have joined restraint, and they are the two that don't veto anyway (France none since 1989) [P9]. Voluntary restraint binds only those who don't need binding.
   - Turn: two permanent members themselves admit the veto should not block action in mass atrocities [P9] - they concede our harm.

6. **"Abolition is impossible (Art. 108 needs P5 ratification)."** - "Should", not "would". Feasibility is not a harm; the judge evaluates whether it is desirable.

7. **"The veto is rarely used; the Council mostly works."** - It is used on the gravest files: Gaza, Syria, Ukraine, DPRK [P5]. Frequency is the wrong metric; one veto on a lifeline for four million [P3] outweighs 50 routine renewals. Plus the threat keeps drafts off the table (analytic, no card).

8. **"Syria aid continued anyway via Damascus's consent."** - Partial concession: yes, but then access runs on the terms of the government the region was fleeing. The Council mandate existed precisely so aid didn't depend on that consent (analytic). Still a harm.

9. **"The veto forces compromise (e.g. the 2024 Ramadan ceasefire resolution passed after a veto)."** - That resolution passed with no veto; under Pro it passes too. Vetoes don't create compromise, they let one country set the price: six US ceasefire vetoes in one war [P4].

## Weighing
- Probability: our harms are a voting record [P2][P3][P4][P5]; their exit/war harm is a prediction, and the best test case (Russia outvoted 141-5 [P7]) shows no exit.
- Scope/magnitude: four million people on one lifeline [P3], famine [P4], and the security guarantee for every non-P5 state.
- Prerequisite: the Council can deliver aid, sanctions or peacekeeping only if it can decide. Decision-making comes first [P6].
- Comparative line for lay judge: "They say the veto keeps the powerful at the table. We say it lets them turn the table over."
