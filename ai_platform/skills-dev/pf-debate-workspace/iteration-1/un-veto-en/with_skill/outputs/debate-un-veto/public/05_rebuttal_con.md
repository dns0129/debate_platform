I'll restate each of Pro's counterexamples fairly, then cut. Our case first.

On our first contention, keeping the giants in the room.

Pro says the League let every member block, and the giants left anyway. True. That proves a veto is not enough, not that it is unnecessary. Davies and Bellamy give the reason: Washington argued a world body must let great powers protect their "vital interests" or fall into irrelevance [C1].

Next, France [P9]. France stopped casting the veto, not holding it. A country that can still say no has exactly the certainty David Bosco describes. And Pro's own card shows the veto can be restrained without being abolished.

Then Pro says the seat is worth more than the veto. Here is the step that fails. In January 2026, Washington decided to withdraw from 31 UN-linked entities, bodies where it had membership but no veto, arguing they were "no longer serving American interests" [C5]. Pro's rebuttal never answered that. A seat without protection is exactly what a great power walks away from today. That step also decides peacekeeping, since Pro agrees with Walter, Howard and Fortna.

And the court order. Pro says Russia lost a binding decision and stayed, so no exit. Agreed. That is the other half of our dilemma: a great power either leaves, or stays and ignores you. Pro just chose "ignores you."

On our second contention, the safety valve.

Pro's third option: the decision binds every other member. But in crossfire Pro conceded their India card does not say what India would do under a Council decision. Binding is not compliance. The only binding decision against a great power in this round got the Kremlin's answer, as the Moscow Times reported: "We can't take that decision into account" [C9].

Then Libya. Patrick's test is war authorized "contrary to the expressed wishes" of a great power [C3]. China and Russia abstained [P8]. They held a veto and chose not to use it. Abstaining is not objecting. Pro's plan creates the case Libya never tested: a great power saying no and being overridden.

Finally, the turn: the system is "rigged." We called it unfair in our first minute. But Patrick's sentence continues: "to reduce the likelihood that major war will break out among them." And Pro's rebuttal admitted a vote would not stop Russia's army. So on Ukraine, abolition changes the paper, not the war, and adds the one risk nobody can undo.

Now their case.

Their first contention: abolish the veto, the resolution passes, help arrives. The cut is at the last step whenever the vetoing power or its ally is a party. A Gaza ceasefire resolution is a demand, not an army. I'll grant that Bab al-Hawa needs no enforcement. But it only passes in a Council the great powers still sit in, which is our first contention.

Their second contention: the accused shouldn't be the judge. Two cuts. First, it isn't unique. Security Council Report wrote in April 2026 that when the Council is gridlocked, the General Assembly "can and has" acted, including establishing the International, Impartial and Independent Mechanism for Syria [C8]. Pro's own 141-to-5 vote is another example [P7]. Second, it turns. Davies and Bellamy, writing in The Conversation, warn that without a veto Russia could influence eight other members "to validate the annexation of Crimea," or the United States could persuade eight states "that no Palestinian state should ever exist" [C10]. The accused doesn't need to judge; it needs eight friends, and under Pro's Article 25, that verdict binds all 193.

So weigh it. Pro says our harm is a prediction and theirs is a voting record. But a record of votes is not a record of results. Their impact needs a vote against a great power to change what that power does, and the only evidence on that step says it doesn't. Ours comes first: no Council with the giants inside, no resolution at all. Please negate.
