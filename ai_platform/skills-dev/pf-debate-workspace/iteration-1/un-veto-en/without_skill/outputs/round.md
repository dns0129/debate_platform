## 1. Pro Constructive, Amara Okafor (4:00) {#pro-constructive}

<div class="speech pro" markdown="1">

On September 18th of last year, fourteen of the fifteen members of the Security Council voted to demand a ceasefire in Gaza, and one vote killed it. In November 2024, fourteen of fifteen voted for a ceasefire in Sudan, which aid agencies call the world's worst humanitarian crisis. One vote killed that too. This April, eleven members voted to protect shipping through the Strait of Hormuz, a route that carries about a fifth of the world's oil. Two votes killed it. My partner and I affirm: *Resolved: The United Nations should abolish the permanent members' veto power in the Security Council.*

**Two observations.** First, what abolition means. Article 27(3) of the Charter says substantive decisions need nine votes "including the concurring votes of the permanent members." We remove that clause. Decisions need nine of fifteen. The five permanent members **keep their permanent seats**. They just can't block alone anymore. Second, this is a *should* resolution. Under Article 108, the amendment passes and is ratified. We're debating the world after abolition, not whether Moscow would agree to it today.

**Framework.** Article 24 gives the Council "primary responsibility for the maintenance of international peace and security" and says it acts on behalf of every member state. So weigh the round by one question: which world lets the Council protect more human lives?

**Contention One: The veto is a license for impunity.**

*Subpoint A: The aggressor judges its own case.* On February 25, 2022, Russia vetoed a resolution that deplored Russia's own invasion of Ukraine. The Charter makes a party to a dispute abstain on Chapter VI votes, but not on Chapter VII enforcement votes. So the country on trial casts the deciding vote.

*Subpoint B: It shields atrocities.* Take Syria. Between 2011 and 2023, Russia vetoed well over a dozen Syria resolutions. With China, it blocked a 2014 referral of Syria to the International Criminal Court. In 2019 and 2020 it used vetoes and veto threats to cut the UN's cross-border aid crossings from four to one. Then in July 2023 it vetoed the last one, Bab al-Hawa, the lifeline for more than four million people in northwest Syria.

> **UN Human Rights Office, June 2022:** An estimated 306,887 civilians were killed in Syria between March 2011 and March 2021. That averages 83 civilian deaths a day for ten years.

Take Sudan. After Russia's November 2024 veto, Security Council Report found the Council largely limited to press statements on Sudan's war through 2025. In October 2025 the city of El Fasher fell to the RSF after an 18-month siege, followed by reports of mass killings.

*Subpoint C: It isn't partisan. It's structural.* The United States has vetoed six Gaza ceasefire drafts since October 2023. Russia and China vetoed the Hormuz resolution this April. Every permanent member uses the veto to protect its friends. The problem is the power, not any one country.

*Subpoint D: The hidden veto.* Counting vetoes undercounts the damage.

> **Security Council Report, August 2026 mid-year review:** In the first half of 2026 a draft on de-mining was withdrawn before a vote because China and Russia were expected to oppose it. The risk of a veto continues to deter Council action on contentious issues.

The impact: when the Council *can* act, lives are saved.

> **Hultman, Kathman & Shannon, *American Journal of Political Science*, 2013:** Using monthly data on African conflicts from 1991 to 2008, they find that as the UN sends more troops and police to a mission, fewer civilians are killed. Deploying several thousand troops cuts civilian killings dramatically.

Every mandate the veto blocks is a mission that never deploys.

**Contention Two: The veto is draining the Council's legitimacy.**

> **Security Council Report, January 2026:** The Council adopted 44 resolutions in 2025, the fewest since 1991 and the fifth straight year of decline. Only 61.4% were unanimous. The average from 2014 to 2023 was 83.9%.

In 2024 there were seven vetoed drafts, the most since 1986. States have started routing around the Council. Since 2022, the "veto initiative" in General Assembly resolution 76/262 has forced the Assembly to debate every veto, and it had done so 14 times by August 2026. In July 2026, Malaysia renewed its call to abolish the veto outright. Voluntary restraint has already failed: 130 states back the ACT code of conduct on atrocity vetoes, but only two of the five permanent members, France and the UK, have signed it.

A Council nobody trusts is a Council nobody obeys. Abolition gives the Council what it needs to be credible: decisions that rest on a broad majority, not on one capital's permission.

We're proud to affirm, and I'm open for crossfire.

</div>

## 2. Con Constructive, Priya Natarajan (4:00) {#con-constructive}

<div class="speech con" markdown="1">

In 1945 at the San Francisco Conference, the small states wanted to abolish the veto. Senator Tom Connally of Texas stood up and tore a copy of the draft Charter in half in front of them. His point: go home and tell people you defeated the veto, then explain where the Charter went. He wasn't bluffing. No veto meant no United Nations. The question in this round isn't whether the veto is fair. It isn't. The question is whether the UN is **better off** without it, and my partner and I negate because it isn't.

**Framework.** Weigh realistic consequences: the UN the day after abolition, not an ideal one. A resolution matters only if someone with power carries it out. Fiat gets the amendment passed. It doesn't make great powers comply with votes they lose.

**Contention One: Abolition pushes great powers out of the UN or teaches them to ignore it.**

*Subpoint A: History.* The League of Nations collapsed when great powers left. The United States never joined. Japan and Germany walked out in 1933, Italy in 1937, and the Soviet Union was expelled in 1939. The UN's founders built the veto so that great powers would never have a reason to leave. For eighty years, none has.

*Subpoint B: The risk is real right now.* The US is already heading for the exits.

> **White House memorandum, January 2026 (reported by JURIST and others):** The United States withdrew from 66 international organizations, 31 of them in the UN system, including the UN climate convention, the IPCC and UN Women.

And the money is already drying up:

> **Security Council Report, January 2026:** As of November 2025, the US owed about $2.37 billion in UN arrears.

Look at the pattern. The US leaves bodies it can't control. It stays in the one body where it holds a veto: the Security Council.

*Subpoint C: The link.* Abolish the veto and, for the first time, a nine-vote majority could condemn the US and Israel for this year's strikes on Iran, or order measures against Russia in Ukraine. Great powers don't comply with decisions against their core interests. In March 2022 the International Court of Justice ordered Russia to suspend its military operations in Ukraine. The order was legally binding, and Russia ignored it.

The impact is a lose-lose. If they **leave**, the UN loses its biggest funders. The US pays 26% of peacekeeping costs and China nearly 24%. In October 2025 the UN already had to cut about a quarter of its peacekeepers over funding shortfalls. The UN would also lose the only permanent table where Washington, Beijing and Moscow have to talk. If they **stay and defy**, every ignored resolution teaches the world that Council decisions are optional.

**Contention Two: Majority rule turns the Council into a vote market and a war-authorizing machine.**

*Subpoint A: Vote-buying.*

> **Kuziemko & Werker, *Journal of Political Economy*, 2006:** When a country rotates onto the Security Council, its US aid rises 59% and its UN aid 8%. The effect spikes in years with important votes and tracks exactly when the country joins and leaves the Council.

Today a bought vote matters only at the margins, because any permanent member can still veto. Without the veto, the ten elected members decide everything. Nine votes becomes legal authority, and the Council's decisions go to whoever pays most.

*Subpoint B: War authorization.* There has been one moment when the Council effectively worked without a Soviet veto. In 1950 the USSR was boycotting the Council, and the Council authorized a war against a Soviet client in Korea. Chinese troops entered, and millions died. Now look at this April. The Hormuz draft would have encouraged states to coordinate military escorts in the middle of a war the US and Israel were fighting with Iran. Without a veto, whichever bloc gets nine votes can have the Council bless force against a rival's partner. That's how a regional war turns into a great-power war.

The veto is ugly. It's also part of why the UN has lasted eighty years without its permanent members fighting each other directly. We're proud to negate.

</div>

## 4. Pro Rebuttal, Daniel Reyes (4:00) {#pro-rebuttal}

<div class="speech pro" markdown="1">

I'll go straight to their case and then weigh.

**Their Contention One: exit and defiance.** Seven responses.

**One, fiat and Article 108.** A Charter amendment only takes effect once it's ratified by two-thirds of member states *including all five permanent members*. So in the world where we abolish the veto, Washington, Beijing, Moscow, London and Paris have each *consented*. Countries don't ratify an amendment and then storm out over it.

**Two, it's non-unique.** Their own evidence proves the veto doesn't keep the US engaged. The US left 66 bodies in January *with its Security Council veto fully intact*. What drives US withdrawal is domestic politics, not voting rules. Meanwhile the US stays in the General Assembly, where it has no veto and loses lopsided votes every year.

**Three, the League analogy flips.** The League Council ran on unanimity, which means *every* member had a veto. It still collapsed. So the League's lesson isn't "give great powers a veto." It's that an institution that can't act becomes irrelevant, and irrelevant institutions get abandoned. That's the path the Council is on today, and it's our Contention Two.

**Four, defiance still beats silence.** Some resolutions will be ignored. But a resolution is a legal basis for sanctions, for coalitions, and for aid delivery. Take the Hormuz draft. The *Gulf states themselves* wrote it, with Jordan: Bahrain, Qatar, Kuwait, Saudi Arabia and the UAE. It would have given them legal cover to escort their own ships. Iran defying that resolution doesn't make it worthless. It gives everyone else permission to act.

**Five, the only great-power walkout in UN history proves our point.** In January 1950 the Soviet Union walked out of the Council. It watched the Council act on Korea without it, and by August it was back in its chair. Empty chairs lose votes. For a great power, leaving is the worst option, and that's even more true once there's no veto to hide behind, because staying in the room is the only way left to shape the outcome.

**Six, the funding impact is backwards.** The US doesn't pay for peacekeeping as charity.

> **Government Accountability Office, GAO-18-243, 2018:** A US-run operation comparable to the UN mission in the Central African Republic would have cost about $5.7 billion. That's more than twice the UN's cost, and almost eight times the roughly $700 million the US actually contributed.

Peacekeeping is a bargain for Washington, and nobody walks away from a bargain because they lost a vote.

**Seven, turn: the veto *causes* great-power aggression.** Russia invaded Ukraine knowing the Council could never respond, because it holds the veto. Impunity invites aggression. Take the veto away and every permanent member has to weigh a real cost before acting.

**Their Contention Two, Subpoint A, vote-buying.** Three responses. It's non-unique: Natarajan conceded in crossfire that it happens today. Competing bidders cancel each other out. And even a resolution with some bought votes needs nine countries from different regions, which is far more legitimate than one country blocking fourteen.

**Their Contention Two, Subpoint B, war authorization.** Three responses.

First, nine votes means cross-bloc support. This April, Colombia and Pakistan abstained on Hormuz. The elected members aren't rubber stamps.

Second, Korea is a bad example for them. North Korea invaded the South. The Council authorized collective defense against aggression, which is exactly what the Charter exists to do. The war would have happened with or without a UN vote. The veto would only have taken away the legitimacy.

Third, nuclear deterrence, not Article 27, has kept the permanent members out of direct war. The veto existed through Korea, Vietnam and Afghanistan, and proxy wars killed millions anyway. The veto doesn't prevent wars between great powers. It only prevents the Council from responding to them.

**Weighing.** Our harms are *certain* and *happening now*: in Syria, in Sudan, in Gaza, in Hormuz. Their harm is a *speculative* walkout that has never happened once in eighty years, by countries that would have just ratified the change. Probability favors us, and so does the timeframe. Please affirm.

</div>

## 5. Con Rebuttal, Theo Lindqvist (4:00) {#con-rebuttal}

<div class="speech con" markdown="1">

Their case first, then back to ours.

**Their Contention One: impunity.** Five responses.

**One, no solvency: the problem is enforcement, not votes.** Look at what happens to resolutions that *pass*. In June 2024, Resolution 2736 demanded that the RSF lift the siege of El Fasher. It passed 14 to 0, and El Fasher fell in October 2025 anyway, followed by mass killings. In March 2024, Resolution 2728 demanded an immediate Gaza ceasefire. It passed with the US abstaining, and the war went on for more than a year. The General Assembly condemned Russia 141 to 5, and Russia kept going. A vote doesn't stop a tank.

**Two, the UN has no army.** The Charter's Article 43 agreements to give the Council standing forces were never concluded. Enforcement comes from volunteers and funders, and you can't vote the US and China into paying for missions they opposed. They're half the peacekeeping budget. Their Hultman study looks at missions that had consent and funding. It says nothing about missions forced through over a permanent member's objection.

**Three, a double bind.** After abolition, the Council either confronts great powers or it doesn't. If it does, by authorizing action against Russia in Ukraine or against the US and Israel over Iran, it risks direct war between nuclear powers. If it doesn't dare, then Ukraine, Gaza and Hormuz look exactly the same as today. Either way, their headline examples aren't solved.

**Four, the Libya turn.** In 2011 Russia and China abstained on Resolution 1973 to protect civilians in Libya. NATO turned "protect civilians" into regime change. That is *why* Moscow and Beijing started vetoing on Syria. Majority rule means more Libyas, which means more backlash and less cooperation.

**Five, on Hormuz.** Russia argued the draft left out the US-Israeli strikes that started the war. Whether or not you agree, the veto stopped the Council from taking sides in a war where one of its own permanent members was fighting. And note that the Council *did* act. On March 11, Resolution 2817 condemned Iran's attacks on the Gulf states, 13 to 0, with China and Russia abstaining. The Council acts when there's enough consensus.

And on the **hidden veto**: it's also a hidden compromise. Drafts get negotiated until all fifteen members can live with them. That's how 2817 passed 13 to 0. A Council that has to find consensus writes resolutions that everyone at least tolerates.

**Their Contention Two: legitimacy.** Three responses. First, output is falling because the great powers disagree, not because of Article 27. Remove the veto and they still disagree; they just stop listening. Second, the status quo is already adding accountability without touching the Charter. Since 2022, every veto triggers a General Assembly debate, and there have been fourteen so far. A country that vetoes now has to defend it in front of all 193 members. That's reform without the risk of exit. Third, a turn. The African Union's Ezulwini Consensus opposes the veto *in principle*, but says that as long as it exists, new African permanent members should get it too. The Global South wants a seat at the veto table. It doesn't want a Council where nine votes can be bought.

**Now back to our case.**

On **fiat**: ratification is one moment. Resolutions keep coming for decades. The Senate that ratifies in 2026 isn't the Senate that gets outvoted in 2031.

On **"the US left 66 bodies anyway"**: that's our point. The US leaves bodies where it lacks control and stays in the one where it has a veto. Their answer about the General Assembly doesn't work, because the Assembly can't bind the US. The Council can.

On the **1950 walkout**: think about why the Soviets came back. They came back to use their veto. Within weeks Moscow was vetoing Korea resolutions, which is why the General Assembly invented "Uniting for Peace" that November. Take away the veto and there's nothing to come back for.

On **the GAO card**: peacekeeping is a bargain only if Washington controls where the troops go. A Council that can authorize missions over US objections is a Council Congress can defund, which it did with UN dues in the 1980s and 1990s.

On **deterrence**: deterrence prevents deliberate war. The veto prevents the UN from sleepwalking into one. Korea shows it: a Council authorization against a great power's client brought Chinese troops into the war.

On **vote-buying**: they say competition cancels out. It doesn't. It means the richest bidder wins.

Negate.

</div>

## 7. Pro Summary, Amara Okafor (3:00) {#pro-summary}

<div class="speech pro" markdown="1">

Two things decide this round: which world is more likely, and where the harms actually are.

**First, their case: exit.** Extend three responses. **One**, under fiat all five permanent members ratified the amendment. They *chose* a Council without the veto. **Two**, their own evidence is non-unique. The US left 66 bodies in January with its veto fully intact, so the veto isn't what keeps the US engaged. **Three**, no permanent member has left the UN in eighty years. Russia lost 141 to 5 and stayed. The one great-power walkout in history, the Soviets in 1950, lasted seven months, because empty chairs lose votes. Theo says they came back for the veto. Under our side they come back for the same reason they'd stay in the first place: a permanent seat and a vote. A great power that walks out just hands the Council to its rivals. Their exit story is a prediction that has never once come true.

And on money: the GAO found a US-run version of the Central African Republic mission would cost almost eight times what the US actually paid the UN. Peacekeeping is a bargain, and bargains don't get abandoned over one lost vote.

Vote-buying is dead. Priya conceded it happens today. War authorization is answered too: nine votes needs cross-bloc support, and Colombia and Pakistan abstaining on Hormuz shows the elected members aren't rubber stamps.

**Second, our case.** They answer it with two arguments.

*"Resolutions don't work."* Extend Daniel's answer from crossfire: that's selection bias. The drafts that died were the ones with teeth, like the ICC referral, the North Korea sanctions panel and the aid mandates. The drafts that passed, like 2736, were stripped of enforcement *to get past a veto*. You can't point to toothless resolutions as proof that resolutions don't work when the veto is the thing that pulled their teeth.

*The double bind.* There's a third option they never answered: crises where no permanent member is fighting. Theo admitted in crossfire that Russia wouldn't go to war with the UN over a Sudan resolution. That's where the veto has done its worst damage. Look at Bab al-Hawa: aid to four million people that needed no army, only an authorization, and Russia's veto ended the mandate. Look at the North Korea sanctions panel, killed by one veto in March 2024. It was a group of monitors. It needed no soldiers.

On Libya, the veto existed in 2011 and didn't prevent what happened. That's an argument against Resolution 1973, not against majority voting.

**Weighing.** Our harms are **certain**: 306,887 civilians killed in Syria, El Fasher, six vetoed Gaza ceasefires, and a closed shipping lane this spring. Their harm is **speculative**, since it has never happened in eighty years. Our harms are **happening now**, while theirs lives in a hypothetical 2031. On **scope**, the veto hurts people in every region. Their harm is the hurt feelings of five governments that already agreed to the change. Affirm.

</div>

## 8. Con Summary, Priya Natarajan (3:00) {#con-summary}

<div class="speech con" markdown="1">

This round comes down to one question: what happens to the UN when great powers lose control of it? I'll extend our first contention, show that their case isn't solved, and then weigh.

**Our Contention One.** The League collapsed when great powers left. The UN's founders built the veto so that wouldn't happen again, and for eighty years it hasn't.

Now look at the argument they never answered directly. **The US leaves bodies it can't control and stays in the one where it has a veto.** In January 2026 it withdrew from 66 organizations, and it owes about $2.37 billion in arrears. Here are their answers:

- *"The US stays in the General Assembly."* The Assembly can't bind the US. The Council can. Binding power is what triggers exit, and we've said that twice.
- *"Non-unique, since the US left 66 bodies with the veto intact."* That proves our link. Those were bodies *without* a US veto. The one body with a veto is the one the US hasn't touched.
- *"They ratified."* Ratification is one moment. Resolutions come for decades. The first time nine votes condemn a US ally, the calculation changes. They never answered that.
- *"1950 proves they come back."* The Soviets came back to *veto*, and they started doing it within weeks. Their own example shows what brings great powers back to the table, and it's the thing they want to abolish.
- *"Peacekeeping is a bargain."* Only while Washington decides where the troops go. Congress withheld UN dues for years in the 1980s and 1990s over far less than being outvoted on a war.

The impact: either the US leaves and takes 26% of peacekeeping with it, when peacekeepers were already cut by a quarter last year, or it stays and ignores the Council, the way Russia ignored the ICJ.

**Their case.** They solve the headline examples.

- **The double bind.** Their "third option" concedes the double bind for Ukraine, Gaza and Hormuz, which were the first three examples in their constructive. In those cases the Council either risks a war between nuclear powers or does nothing.

## 10. Pro Final Focus, Daniel Reyes (2:00) {#pro-ff}

<div class="speech pro" markdown="1">

Three reasons to affirm.

You can't use a hypothetical walkout to cancel out real people who are dying today. Ours is happening now: Syria's 306,887, El Fasher, six Gaza vetoes, Hormuz. Certain harms now beat speculative harms later. And think about who pays for each world. In theirs, it's the four million people waiting on a convoy.
